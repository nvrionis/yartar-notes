# Cael

Half-elf, mid-thirties. Caravan guard turned wandering hedge-priest of **Tymora**. Travels light, hires on by the run, blesses a crossing and moves along. No surname — road-folk often haven't one, and he's never offered.

## Where he comes from

His father was **eladrin**: one season in a human woman's life, gone before Cael was old enough to have a face for him. He answers questions about it the way half-elves do — a shrug, and *he wasn't from here.* It's why he reads as fey to anything that looks, and he says so before he's asked. It saves trouble on temple thresholds.

His mother was human, and she is dead. The hill hamlet is not there any more — not burned, not cursed, just **emptied**, the way small places in the North empty when the young leave and the old die and nobody writes it down. He doesn't name it.

## The road

Fourteen or so when he took up with caravans, and he never took up with anything else. **[[Ghesh]]** taught him the guard's trade. Twenty years behind him now, most of it out of [[Yartar]], and it is the part of his life anyone can check, because all of it happened in front of witnesses.

## The night the coin turned

He tells this one short, which is how people know it's true.

A bad night on a bad road. The caravan came apart in the dark and he walked out of it at dawn — the only one on his side of the wagons who did. When the sun came up, the dark had stopped being a place he could see into, and it never came back.

A half-elf with no night-eyes is a strange thing and he doesn't pretend otherwise. He calls it **the toll**: the Lady took his night and left him the rest of his life. He has never once argued the trade.

## What he preaches, which is not much

No temple, no rite, nothing asked for a blessing. Luck, as he tells it, isn't fortune — it's the moment before a thing has settled, when it could still go either way. A blessing is just a way of getting a man to believe the moment is his. Belief moves the outcome.

**On his magic:** the misdirections and the doubled shapes get the same explanation — luck is a shell game, and the Lady likes a crowd that isn't sure yet. Nobody has pushed harder than that, because a road-priest doing a trick with light is a man earning his supper.
